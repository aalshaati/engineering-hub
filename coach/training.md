# Training Progress Principles

How the coach reads the Hevy lifting log. Companion to `principles.md`:
that file governs diet and the scale; this one governs the weights.

`principles.md` Rules 4 and 6 both fire on "strength dropping week-over-week
on key lifts" but never define it. This file is that definition. When the
coach decides whether a diet break or ease-off is warranted, the strength
half of that decision comes from here.

---

## 0. WHAT THE DATA CAN AND CANNOT TELL US

The only reliable signals in Hevy are **weight, reps, and set type**, plus
the **target rep range** on the routine. That's it.

- **RPE is never logged** — the coach must never ask "what was your RPE" or
  reason as if effort data exists. Infer effort from set type (`failure`)
  and from reps landing at the bottom vs. top of the target range.
- **No per-set notes, no in-workout bodyweight.** Bodyweight for relative-
  strength reasoning comes from `health:daily`, not Hevy.
- **Exclude `warmup` sets from all progress judgments.** Only `normal` and
  `failure` sets count as working sets.
- Compare a lift only against **the same exercise** in earlier sessions —
  never across different exercises, even similar ones.

---

## 1. KEY LIFTS ARE THE SIGNAL — ACCESSORIES ARE NOISE

Not every lift carries equal weight in a verdict.

- **Key lifts (the signal):** the heavy compounds — Bench Press, Squat,
  Romanian Deadlift, Overhead Press, Bent-Over Row, Lat Pulldown, Pull-Up.
  These are stable, repeated week to week, and respond slowly. A real trend
  on these means something.
- **Accessories (mostly noise):** lateral raises, cable flyes, curls,
  pushdowns, calf raises. Reps here swing on pump, fatigue, and order in the
  session. **A dip on an accessory is not a stall** and should never, on its
  own, trigger a diet break or ease-off.

When `principles.md` says "key lifts," it means this first list.

---

## 2. READING PROGRESSION — DOUBLE PROGRESSION INSIDE THE REP RANGE

Each routine exercise has a target rep range (e.g. Bench 6–10, Lat Pulldown
12–15). Judge progress against that range, in this order:

1. **Add reps first.** Same weight, more reps inside the range = progressing.
   (185 lb × 6 → 185 lb × 8 is progress.)
2. **Then add weight.** Once all working sets hit the *top* of the range,
   next session go up in weight and drop back toward the bottom. That weight
   jump is the clearest progression signal there is.
3. **Bodyweight lifts (Pull-Up, Dips) progress by reps only** — weight is
   null, so total reps across working sets is the whole story.

To collapse weight and reps into one comparable number, use **estimated 1RM**
(Epley): `e1RM = weight × (1 + reps/30)`. Track the top working set's e1RM
across sessions — a rising line is progress regardless of whether it came
from reps or load. Caveat: e1RM is trustworthy in the **5–12 rep** range. For
higher-rep accessory work, just compare reps at a fixed weight.

**The three verdicts:**
- **Progressing** — e1RM up, or more reps at the same load, or moved up in
  load, vs. the last 1–2 sessions of that lift.
- **Maintaining** — same load, same reps, holding inside the range. In a cut,
  this is a *good* outcome (see Rule 4).
- **Regressing** — fewer reps at the same load, or had to drop the load,
  across **two or more** sessions of that lift. One bad session is not a
  regression.

---

## 3. WHAT A STALL ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

A stall is **not** one flat session. It is:

> the same key lift showing no improvement in reps or load — flat or
> declining e1RM — across **2–3 consecutive sessions** of that lift, while
> still in the same rep range and the same program.

When a genuine stall is confirmed, the coach's response depends on which
phase we're in, and in a deficit the order is deliberately conservative:

1. **Confirm it's real** — 2–3 sessions, not a single off day, and not a
   program change masquerading as a stall (Rule 6).
2. **Check the cheap inputs first** — sleep and protein from `health:daily`.
   A stall riding alongside poor sleep or low protein is a recovery problem,
   not a programming problem.
3. **Hold, don't force.** In a deficit, do **not** chase the stall by adding
   load or volume. Maintaining through a stall is often the correct outcome.
4. **If a key lift is genuinely declining (not just flat) across 2–3
   sessions → that is the strength-drop trigger** in `principles.md` Rules 4
   and 6. The response there is *more food or a diet break*, never "push
   harder." Training and diet agree: the answer to a deficit stall is
   recovery, not intensity.

---

## 4. IN A DEFICIT, MAINTENANCE IS THE WIN

We are in a fat-loss phase (`principles.md` Rule 1: ~0.7–1%/wk). That reframes
every training verdict.

- **The realistic goal in a cut is to hold strength, not build it.** Flat key
  lifts while bodyweight drops means you're getting *relatively* stronger and
  leaner at once. The coach should say so plainly and not treat a plateau as
  failure.
- **Early-cut progress can still happen** — especially on lifts new to the
  current program — but it slows as the deficit accumulates. Expect that.
- **A slow decline late in a cut can be acceptable** *if* bodyweight is still
  falling and the drop is small. Relative strength is preserved.
- **The red line** is a key lift declining **week over week, persistently**.
  That's not "a cut being a cut" — that's under-recovery, and it routes
  straight to `principles.md` Rule 6: ease off, add food. Never the reverse.

The coach never tells Abdulla to train harder to fix a deficit stall.

---

## 5. DIET BREAKS ARE THE WINDOW TO PUSH

`principles.md` Rule 4 schedules 1–2 week diet breaks at maintenance every
6–10 weeks. Those higher-calorie windows are when training should get
*ambitious*, and the two systems reinforce each other:

- **During a break, recovery capacity is highest** → this is the time to
  chase rep PRs and add load on key lifts. Progression attempted here is most
  likely to stick.
- **Right after a break, expect the best numbers** of the cycle. If lifts
  *don't* recover within the 1–2 weeks following a break, that's a signal the
  break was too short or the deficit too deep — feed it back into the next
  diet decision.
- **When a break was triggered *by* falling strength** (Rule 4's trigger),
  watch the key lifts over the following 1–2 weeks as the **confirmation the
  break worked.** Lifts climbing back = break did its job.
- **Don't add training volume mid-deficit and save it for the break.**
  Intensity pushes belong in the high-calorie windows, not the deep ones.

---

## 6. NEVER COMPARE ACROSS A PROGRAM CHANGE

Abdulla switched from the 6-day Advanced Upper/Lower to the current
Push/Pull/Legs on **2026-06-02**. A program change resets the books.

- **A new program is a fresh baseline.** New exercise order, new rep ranges,
  new fatigue distribution. Numbers are not comparable to the old program,
  and an apparent "stall" right after a switch is usually just the new
  baseline settling.
- **Establish baseline over the first 2–3 sessions** of each lift in the new
  program before judging progression or stalls at all.
- **Lifts that exist in both programs** (Bench, Lat Pulldown carried over)
  *can* be compared with caution — but order and surrounding fatigue differ,
  so weight it lightly.
- **As of this file's writing, PPL has under two weeks of data.** It is too
  early to call any PPL stall. The Upper/Lower and 3-Day-Full-Body logs are a
  closed prior chapter, useful for history but not for week-over-week PPL
  verdicts.

## How the coach uses this in a check-in

Slots into the weekly check-in alongside `principles.md`:

1. Pull recent workouts from Hevy (this session and the trailing ~2 weeks).
2. Drop warmup sets; isolate the **key lifts** (Rule 1).
3. For each, compute the top working set's e1RM and compare to that lift's
   last 1–2 sessions **within the current program only** (Rules 2, 6).
4. Classify each: progressing / maintaining / regressing (Rule 2).
5. Read it through the deficit lens — maintenance is a win (Rule 4).
6. If a key lift is genuinely regressing across 2–3 sessions, hand that to
   `principles.md` Rules 4 and 6 as the strength-drop trigger (Rule 3).
7. If we're in or just past a diet break, judge whether lifts are responding
   as expected (Rule 5).

The training verdict feeds the diet decision — it never overrides the safety
guardrail. When in doubt, the answer is recovery, not intensity.
